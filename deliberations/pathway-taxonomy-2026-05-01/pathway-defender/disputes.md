- **Dispute: Classification axis — authorization-mechanism vs. deliberation-requirement**

An orchestrator routing a new amendment does not consult the taxonomy only to learn whether deliberation is required; they consult it to learn *what contract they must satisfy* to invoke the low-cost pathway. That contract is different for rows 3 and 4 (absence of new content vs. prior deliberation scope coverage of deferred wording), and the difference is diagnostically significant. See my modified Rec 2 and my cross-review of pathway-skeptic (Dangerous Contradictions, first item).
  - **Opposing position(s)**: pathway-skeptic modified Rec 1 maintains that "the 'deliberation requirement' axis is the constitutionally operative one, because CONSTITUTION.md's Governance section exists to govern future decisions about what process amendments require." Skeptic concedes the authorization-mechanism distinction has "genuine diagnostic value" but proposes preserving it as a parenthetical sub-note within a collapsed single row, rather than as a separate row with separate routing logic.
  - **Why I will not concede**: The parenthetical-sub-note formulation gives the authorization-mechanism distinction a location but not a function. A sub-note embedded in a single row cannot serve as a routing instruction — it reads as historical context, not as a gate condition. Future orchestrators reading the collapsed row will see "verbatim-preservation contract holds — either (a) structural rearrangement, or (b) application of wording pre-specified verbatim..." and apply the same routing logic to both sub-cases. If the routing logic is the same, sub-case (b)'s additional conditions (scope-coverage requirement, prior SIR provenance) are invisible as a mandatory check — they read as definitional refinements rather than as distinct conditions that a claiming amendment must satisfy affirmatively. This is not a documentation problem; it is an enforcement problem. The gate conditions I formalized in modified Rec 3 require condition (b) (scope coverage) to be satisfied affirmatively, and that condition is only visible as a genuine gate if sub-case (b) has its own row. The Governance section's Removal checklist structure — a separate, enumerated checklist with mandatory steps — is the model here: it exists precisely because embedded prose in a paragraph cannot enforce a sequence of required checks.
  - **Counter-argument to their position**: Skeptic's argument that "an orchestrator routing a new amendment consults the taxonomy to learn whether deliberation is required, not to understand the historical reasons prior amendments avoided it" conflates the taxonomy's routing function with its gate function. The taxonomy does both. Its routing function (which pathway do I use?) is answered correctly by the "deliberation requirement" axis. But its gate function (have I satisfied the conditions that make this pathway legitimate?) is answered only by the authorization-mechanism axis. Skeptic's collapse gives orchestrators the routing answer but obscures the gate answer, which is where pathway abuse occurs. An orchestrator who claims sub-case (b) without satisfying scope coverage will not be checked by a collapsed row's sub-note; they will be checked only if the scope-coverage requirement is visible as a mandatory row-level condition.

If the synthesizer adopts the authorization-mechanism axis, separate rows are structurally necessary and skeptic's collapse conflates two differently-authorized pathways. I commit to accepting the synthesizer's axis choice and following its structural consequence; I cannot accept a formulation that adopts the deliberation-requirement axis for row structure but then insists on scope-coverage conditions that only make sense under the authorization-mechanism axis.

### Final Position Statement

**Non-Negotiables**

1. **The scope-coverage requirement (condition (b)) must be a mandatory gate condition, not an interpretive guideline.** The authorization chain for the pre-ratified pathway must include an affirmative finding that the prior deliberation evaluated the substantive correctness of the deferred wording — not merely that the deliberation authorized a deferral. Without this condition as a hard gate, the pathway is open to the laundering scenario both reviews identified: an orchestrator writes a TODO in a SIR, claims the wording was "implicitly validated" by the prior deliberation, and invokes the low-cost pathway without the prior deliberation having evaluated whether the claim is actually correct. This condition is non-negotiable because the entire value of the pre-ratified pathway rests on the prior deliberation having done the verification work that the current PR is not redoing. If the prior deliberation did not evaluate the substantive claim, the verification work has not been done, and the low-cost pathway is unauthorized regardless of form. Evidence: the self-consistency gap in my new Rec 2 demonstrates that even the canonical example may not satisfy this condition — which is precisely why the condition must be formalized as mandatory rather than left as implicit.

2. **The self-consistency gap in v3.1.2's canonical example must be acknowledged explicitly in the methodology section.** The gateway conditions formalized in both agents' revised recommendations — particularly condition (b) (scope coverage) — may not be satisfied by the v3.1.2 canonical example itself, based on what the governance log as written can support. A pathway whose canonical example may fail its own gate conditions must acknowledge that gap explicitly. Omitting the acknowledgment would leave the pathway's gate conditions appearing self-serving: strict enough to exclude future attempts but not strict enough to require the canonical example to justify itself. This violates Principle XI (Single Source of Truth) as applied to the governance log's record of what the prior deliberation actually evaluated. Evidence: my new Rec 2 and skeptic's new Rec 2 (triggering the same concern from independent analytical directions) confirm the gap is real and must be named, not papered over.

3. **Migration to CONSTITUTION.md is the structural prerequisite for all gate-condition content to carry normative force.** Precisely-stated authorization mechanisms, falsifiable scope-coverage requirements, and explicit proliferation guards are valuable — but only as strong as their normative home. Gate conditions in the governance log are records of intent; the same gate conditions in CONSTITUTION.md's Governance section are rules that future amendments must satisfy. The enrichments in modified Recs 1–4 are governance infrastructure, and governance infrastructure requires a normative home. This is non-negotiable because without migration, the entire Phase 3 concession on scope coverage and gate conditions produces only a more precisely-worded record of intent — not an enforceable constraint. Evidence: unanimous convergence from both agents via independent analytical paths (content argument and authority argument); the normative-authority reconciliation in cross-review (descriptive before migration, normative after).
