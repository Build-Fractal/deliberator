# Cross-Review of Practitioner's Review

Reviewer: skeptic-cross-principle
Subject: practitioner/review.md
Lens: cross-principle structural consistency vs. practitioner's amendment-author operability frame

---

### Dangerous Contradictions

1. **Practitioner Recommendation 3 (delete Criterion 1) directly contradicts the gate's only load-bearing piece.** The practitioner offers "remove Criterion 1 entirely and rely on Criteria 2 and 3" as a defensible aggressive simplification. Cross-principle analysis reaches the opposite conclusion: Criterion 1 (mechanical verification) is the **only** criterion that adds something the existing constitution does not already encode. Criterion 2 is partially subsumed by II's exemplar of falsifiable scope and the constitution's MUST/SHOULD discipline. Criterion 3 is a duplicate of XI (Single Source of Truth) applied to the principle corpus — XI explicitly says "if you find yourself writing the same fact in two places, stop." Deleting Criterion 1 inverts the gate's value proposition: it preserves the redundant criteria and removes the novel one. This is a dangerous recommendation because it would convert a useful (if poorly enforced) gate into pure restatement of XI plus a re-emphasis of MUST/SHOULD discipline. The practitioner's own Recommendation 2 (tighten Criterion 1's path-to-check requirement) is the right move; Recommendation 3 should be withdrawn.

2. **Practitioner Recommendation 6 ("footnote: X, XVI, prose-of-IX would not satisfy Criterion 1") creates a documented permanent two-tier constitution.** The practitioner proposes naming specific grandfathered principles that would fail the gate and instructing future authors not to cite them as precedent. This is an explicit codification of inconsistency — the constitution would simultaneously hold that (a) Criterion 1 is required for new principles and (b) named existing principles fail Criterion 1 and remain in force indefinitely. Principle XI's own logic ("when two sources disagree, it is always a bug — and the fix is always to eliminate the duplicate, not reconcile it") forbids precisely this pattern. The right move is a time-boxed audit (migrate or document permanent exception), not a footnote that institutionalizes the inconsistency. The practitioner's framing optimizes for amendment-author UX at the cost of constitutional coherence.

3. **Practitioner Recommendation 9 ("body-bloat is acceptable; prefer many focused principles when extension > one paragraph") contradicts Criterion 3's whole purpose AND Principle XI.** The practitioner correctly identifies a tension (Principle IX's v2.3.0 Extension is large; refinements going into bodies create giant principles) but resolves it by encouraging *more new principles* when extensions get long. This re-opens the door Criterion 3 was designed to close. Worse, it creates a quantitative threshold ("more than one paragraph") that has no falsifiable scope — exactly the trap Criterion 2 was meant to prevent. A reviewer can wave through any "extension is too long, let's spawn a new principle" argument under this rule. Cross-principle: this would dissolve Criterion 3 into a soft preference, leaving XI as the only barrier against principle-corpus duplication, which the practitioner's other recommendations also weaken.

4. **Practitioner Recommendation 5 (mandate non-author maintainer reviewer) is operationally correct but creates an unaddressed governance gap.** The practitioner closes the rubber-stamp gap with "MUST be reviewed by at least one project maintainer who is not the amendment author." But the constitution does not currently define "project maintainer" — there is no roster, no role designation, no acceptance criteria. Adding a procedural requirement that depends on a role the constitution does not establish creates an enforcement loophole worse than the current self-assessment: any ambiguity about who counts as a maintainer becomes grounds for procedural rejection or (worse) self-designation. Either the constitution must establish the maintainer role (a separate amendment), or the recommendation should specify the artifact (CODEOWNERS file, GitHub team) that resolves the ambiguity.

### Tensions

1. **Practitioner's "PR template + CI lint" mechanization (Rec 1) vs. constitution's silence on tooling.** The practitioner is right that procedural scaffolding is the highest-leverage fix, but the constitution itself does not currently mandate any CI lints or PR templates as governance artifacts. Mandating one for amendments creates an asymmetry: the gate would have stronger enforcement than principles XII, XIII, XXII §1 (all of which say "the linter SHOULD eventually check"). Cross-principle: if the gate gets a CI lint, those principles deserve the same upgrade. The practitioner doesn't address this asymmetry — and it would mean the gate has stricter enforcement than the principles it gates.

2. **Practitioner identifies "operational tuning embedded in constitution" (XVIII's 8-12k token budget, XXV's CI configuration) but doesn't trace the implication.** The practitioner correctly notes the boundary leaks. The cross-principle implication: if XVIII and XXV contain operational tuning, those passages should migrate per the gate's own logic — but the prospective-only carve-out forecloses migration without an explicit act. The practitioner names the symptom and proposes (Rec 6) a footnote, but the deeper question — whether the constitution should host operational tuning at all — is left untouched. This is the "two-tier permanent inconsistency" trap revisited from a different angle.

3. **Practitioner Recommendation 8 (sunset clause for "linter SHOULD eventually" hedges) is sound but applies retroactively to grandfathered text.** The practitioner proposes that any principle relying on a future linter must cite a tracking spec/issue OR accept code-review-only enforcement. But XII, XIII, XXVI all use "the linter SHOULD eventually check" without tracking specs. Applying this rule prospectively-only repeats the gate's grandfathering problem at a finer grain; applying it retroactively requires an audit of existing principles. The recommendation is correct in spirit but the practitioner doesn't pick a side on retroactivity, which is exactly the move that lets governance drift accumulate.

4. **Practitioner's Criterion 3 worked-example proposal (Rec 4) is helpful but reveals a deeper unsolved problem.** The example "Principle XXVIII: All template variables must be lowercase" → composes from IX + XI is a clean rejection. But the harder cases — refinements that extend an existing principle's *scope* (e.g., XXVII operator-subtraction extending XV plugin-isolation) — are not covered by composition tests. XXVII's origin note explicitly says it was promoted from "XV extension" to standalone principle by an arbiter ruling. Under the practitioner's worked example, XXVII could have been refused as compose-able from XV. The example would educate authors but doesn't resolve the boundary it's meant to illuminate.

5. **Practitioner's framing of friction cost (Off-Base Assumption 4, Missed Opportunity 6) treats velocity as a constraint on governance.** The practitioner argues the gate's friction lands asymmetrically on conscientious authors. Cross-principle: this framing prioritizes amendment throughput over corpus integrity, which inverts the gate's stated purpose. The constitution explicitly chose MAJOR/MINOR/PATCH versioning for amendments — i.e., it *wants* friction proportional to impact. The practitioner's "asymmetric friction is bad governance" claim is a values argument, not a structural one, and treating it as a missed opportunity assumes the project's preference is for low-friction amendments. The constitution itself suggests otherwise.

### Safe Agreements

1. **The gate has no specified enforcer / no CI hook — this is the largest single gap.** Practitioner Missed Opportunity 2 and Recommendation 1 align with cross-principle Off-Base Assumption 4 and Recommendation 6. Both reviews independently arrived at the conclusion that the gate is unverifiable in its current form. This is the strongest convergent finding.

2. **Criterion 1's "concrete enough to sketch in one paragraph" is itself unverifiable and recurses the problem Criterion 2 was meant to solve.** Practitioner Missed Opportunity 3 and Recommendation 2 align with cross-principle Missed Opportunity 5 and Recommendation 2. Both propose replacing the prose requirement with a structural requirement (named artifact / labeled `Verification:` block). The practitioner's "name the artifact (file path, test pattern, schema constraint)" and the cross-principle "Verification block with check type + artifact + failure signal" are the same fix expressed at slightly different grain.

3. **Several existing principles (X, XVI, prose of IX, plus VI / XX / XXI in the cross-principle reading) would fail Criterion 1 if proposed today.** Practitioner Missed Opportunity 1 and cross-principle Missed Opportunity 2-4 converge on this finding. Both reviews flag the same precedent risk: the grandfather clause covers the legal question but not the precedent question. The practitioner's list (X, XVI, IX-prose) is a strict subset of the cross-principle list (which adds VI, XX, XXI) — no contradiction, just different coverage.

4. **The "operational guidance" boundary leaks in the existing constitution (XVIII's token budget, XXV's CI configuration).** Practitioner Missed Opportunity 7 and cross-principle Off-Base Assumption 1 agree that the constitution-vs-operational-guidance distinction is cleaner in theory than in the corpus. Both reviews treat this as evidence that the gate's enforcement will produce uneven results without explicit migration criteria.
