# Cross-Review of Practitioner's Review — by skeptic-mathematical

Audited the practitioner's review against my own logical-coherence audit of the Constitutional Inclusion Criteria gate. The practitioner reads the gate from the amendment-author seat; I read it from the formal-coherence seat. Below: where our findings actively contradict each other, where they pull in different directions without contradicting, and where we converge.

---

### Dangerous Contradictions

1. **Criterion 1 collapse vs. Criterion 1 simplification.** My review argues Criterion 1 collapses logically into Criterion 2 — the AND-structure double-counts the same falsifiability property — and recommends merging the two gates (or deleting Criterion 1 outright). The practitioner's recommendations 2 and 3 also flag Criterion 1 but propose *retaining* it as a "name a concrete artifact location" gate distinct from falsifiability. These are not compatible: if Criterion 1 is logically entailed by Criterion 2, naming a file path does not rescue independence — it just decorates the redundant gate with a procedural artifact. Whichever framing wins, the other framing is wrong. A constitution editor who reads both reviews will get conflicting marching orders on the central structural question of the gate.

2. **The grandfathering remedy.** I propose classifying I-XXVII into "exemplar" vs "tolerated" tiers so future authors have a calibration set. The practitioner's recommendation 6 proposes a footnote saying "X, XVI, and prose subsections of IX would not satisfy Criterion 1 under strict reading … should not be cited as precedent." On the surface these agree; on closer read they conflict. My version preserves the full ratification status of all 27 principles and just adds a quality signal. The practitioner's version functionally demotes specific principles from precedential force without going through the "separate intentional act" migration path the constitution itself mandates. That second-class-citizenship-by-footnote is exactly the kind of side-channel demotion the prospective-only carve-out was drafted to prevent. The two recommendations look complementary but one of them violates the constitution's own stated migration discipline.

3. **Enforcer specification vs. mechanization.** The practitioner's recommendation 5 ("at least one project maintainer who is not the amendment author MUST review") names a human enforcer. My recommendation 10 (and recommendation 1 from the practitioner) proposes a structured PR template / mechanical lint as the enforcement surface. These are not the same intervention — they answer different questions. A maintainer-review requirement without a template degenerates into "did someone approve" rubber-stamping. A template without a named human reviewer degenerates into "did the bot pass" rubber-stamping. Adopting one without the other is worse than adopting neither, because it creates the appearance of governance without the substance. The two reviews collectively imply both are needed, but neither review states the dependency, so a maintainer reading either review in isolation will pick one and ship a half-solution.

4. **Body-bloat vs. vocabulary-novelty test.** The practitioner's recommendation 9 explicitly endorses spawning new principles instead of "v2.X Extension" subsections when the extension exceeds one paragraph — preferring many small principles over few sprawling ones. My recommendation 7 proposes a vocabulary-novelty test for Criterion 3 ("the amendment must introduce a noun phrase not present in any existing principle"). These point in opposite directions. The vocabulary test would *reject* most extensions-promoted-to-principles because extensions, by their nature, share vocabulary with the parent principle. The body-bloat preference would push the same content into new principle slots regardless of vocabulary overlap. A reviewer applying both rules will reject the very amendments the body-bloat preference is trying to enable. One of the two recommendations should be dropped before the gate is rewritten.

### Tensions

1. **Self-assessment as "the largest single gap" (practitioner) vs. "logical incoherence" (mine).** The practitioner identifies the absence of a specified enforcer as the dominant defect; I identify the AND-redundancy of Criteria 1 and 2 as the dominant defect. Both are real, but they prescribe different first edits. If the goal is "make the gate actually applied," procedural scaffolding wins. If the goal is "make the gate coherent on its own terms," structural simplification wins. Constitutional editors will need to choose which defect to fix first; the two reviews do not provide a tiebreaker.

2. **"The gate is overengineered" (practitioner) vs. "the gate is undertight" (mine).** The practitioner argues Criterion 1 demands more than the project actually delivers ("at least one form of automated check MUST be feasible" reads as a higher bar than the existing principles meet). I argue Criterion 1 is too loose because a fluent author can sketch a vacuous one-paragraph check for anything. Both framings can be correct simultaneously — the criterion is over-demanding in letter and under-demanding in spirit — but the remediations conflict. Loosening the bar (practitioner) and tightening the artifact requirement (mine) would push the criterion in opposite directions.

3. **Operational-guidance venues: stable home or unstable demotion?** The practitioner's off-base assumption #3 raises a real concern that `CONTRIBUTING.md`, `SKILL.md`, and reference documents have their own version histories and ownership and may be *less* stable than the constitution. My alignment bullet treats those venues as appropriate homes without contesting their stability. If the practitioner is right, the gate's "send rejected principles here" mechanism dumps content into shakier ground — which would argue for tightening the gate (fewer rejections) rather than my recommendation to err toward operational guidance (more rejections). We don't directly contradict, but the asymmetry I propose presupposes the venues are stable, which the practitioner credibly questions.

4. **Boundary leak in existing principles.** The practitioner's missed-opportunity #7 and my missed-opportunity on grandfathering boundary cite the same evidence — XXV is largely operational, XVIII embeds 8-12k token budget tuning — but draw different conclusions. The practitioner concludes the boundary is fundamentally leaky in practice. I conclude the boundary is sound but the calibration set is unmarked. If the practitioner is right, no amount of calibration tagging fixes the structural problem; if I'm right, calibration is the fix. These framings cannot both be load-bearing.

5. **Friction cost vs. governance value.** The practitioner's missed-opportunity #6 highlights that the gate creates an asymmetric friction tax on conscientious authors and bypasses bad-faith ones. My alignment bullet on operational-guidance escape hatch implicitly assumes the friction is justified by the routing benefit. If the friction selection effect is as bad as the practitioner argues, my "err toward operational guidance" recommendation makes the selection effect *worse* by raising the bar for admission. The two reviews need to negotiate which side of the false-admit / false-reject asymmetry the gate should land on.

### Safe Agreements

1. **Mechanize via PR template.** Practitioner recommendation 1 and my recommendation 10 independently arrive at the same fix: convert the gate from prose criteria into a structured PR template with required sections corresponding to each criterion, plus a CI lint that fails when sections are empty on PRs that add new `### N` headings. This is the highest-confidence, lowest-risk change in either review and should be the first concrete edit to the constitution.

2. **Worked rejection example is missing and load-bearing.** Practitioner recommendation 4 and my recommendation 3 both call for a worked example showing a hypothetical principle being rejected by the gate. Practitioner frames it as a Criterion 3 illustration; I frame it as a Mechanical Falsifiability illustration. Either framing satisfies the underlying need — without at least one rejection demonstration, the gate cannot calibrate its users. Add an example and pick whichever criterion it best illustrates.

3. **The "check does not have to exist at amendment time" carve-out is a debt sink.** My missed-opportunity bullet on the indefinite "eventually" hedge and the practitioner's missed-opportunity #4 ("does not say what happens if the check is never built") and recommendation 8 ("sunset clause for 'linter SHOULD eventually check' provisions") describe the same defect from two angles. Both reviews want the carve-out tightened — either by deadline (mine) or by tracking-spec citation (practitioner). The remedies are compatible and could be combined.

4. **Gate header / framing should lead with enforcement, not criteria.** Practitioner recommendation 10 reorders the section to lead with the PR template and enforcer identity. My recommendation 10 (replace prose gate with template) implies the same reordering. A reader's first question is "how is this applied," and both reviews independently land on the same answer.
