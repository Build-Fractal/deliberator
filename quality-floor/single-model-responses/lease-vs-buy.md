# Lease vs. Buy Analysis: Mid-Size Sedan, 5-Year Horizon

## Summary Recommendation

**Buy the car with a 60-month auto loan.** For your specific profile — 12,000 miles/year, 5-year hold period, cost-optimization priority, and no need for a new car every few years — buying is the clearly better financial path. Leasing's structural advantages (lower monthly payments, warranty coverage) don't offset its total cost penalty over your time horizon.

## Financial Analysis

### Buying: 60-Month Loan

Working with a $35,000 vehicle (midpoint of your range), $5,000 down, and a 760 credit score, you'd qualify for a competitive rate in the 5.0–6.0% APR range on a 60-month term.

| Item | Estimate |
|------|----------|
| Loan amount | $30,000 |
| Monthly payment (5.5% APR, 60 mo) | ~$573 |
| Total interest paid | ~$4,400 |
| Total payments over 60 months | ~$34,400 |
| Sales tax (6% on $35,000, varies by state) | ~$2,100 |
| Registration & fees (5 years) | ~$800 |
| Maintenance (5 years, mostly routine) | ~$3,500 |
| Insurance (5 years, full coverage) | ~$7,500 |
| **Total cost of ownership** | **~$48,300** |
| Estimated vehicle value at 60 months | ~$15,000–$17,000 |
| **Net cost after resale** | **~$31,300–$33,300** |

That monthly payment of ~$573 exceeds your stated $400–500 budget, which I'll address in a moment.

### Leasing: Two Consecutive 36- and 24-Month Leases

Because standard leases run 24–36 months, covering 5 years means signing two leases. Typical lease terms on a $35,000 mid-size sedan with 12,000 miles/year:

| Item | Lease 1 (36 mo) | Lease 2 (24–36 mo) |

| Monthly payment | ~$350–$400 | ~$370–$430 |
| Down / due at signing | ~$2,500 | ~$2,000 |
| Disposition fee (end of lease) | ~$350 | ~$350 |

| Item | Estimate |

### The Bottom Line

Buying costs roughly $31,000–$33,000 net after resale. Leasing costs roughly $37,500–$38,500 with nothing to show for it. That's a **$5,000–$7,000 advantage for buying** over the 5-year period. The gap widens if you keep the purchased car beyond 5 years — every payment-free month after the loan is paid off is pure savings.

## Monthly Cash Flow

This is where leasing looks attractive on the surface. Your budget is $400–500/month, and a lease fits neatly inside that range at ~$350–$400. The purchase loan at ~$573 overshoots.

You have three ways to reconcile this:

1. **Extend the loan to 72 months.** Drops the payment to ~$490/month — inside your budget. You'll pay ~$1,500 more in interest, but total cost still beats leasing. You'd owe slightly more than the car is worth for months 48–60, but since you're planning to hold for 5 years anyway, that's a paper risk, not a real one.

2. **Increase your down payment.** Putting $8,000 down instead of $5,000 brings the 60-month payment to ~$515 — close enough to budget. This obviously requires more cash upfront.

3. **Target the lower end of your price range.** A $32,000 vehicle with $5,000 down at 5.5% over 60 months comes to ~$516/month — within striking distance.

Any combination of these works. The 72-month loan is the simplest lever if the cash and vehicle price are set.

## Flexibility Assessment

You named job relocation and family size changes as potential disruptions. Here's how each option handles them:

**Buying gives you more flexibility, not less.** You own the asset. You can sell it anytime, trade it in, or keep it longer. If you relocate, you drive it to the new city. If you need a larger vehicle for a growing family, you sell or trade — and the equity you've built acts as a down payment on the next vehicle.

**Leasing constrains you.** Early lease termination is expensive — you're typically on the hook for the remaining payments, or you pay a transfer fee to find someone to assume the lease. If your mileage needs change (longer commute after relocation), you're locked into the mileage cap or paying $0.15–$0.25/mile for overages. If you need a different vehicle class mid-lease, you're stuck or paying to get out.

The one scenario where leasing flexibility wins: if you become uncertain about whether you want a car at all (major move to a transit-friendly city). Walking away from a lease at term end is simpler than selling. But you specified a 5-year plan in a suburban commuter setting — that scenario is unlikely to apply.

## Maintenance and Repair Risk

Leasing keeps you under the manufacturer's warranty for the full term (most bumper-to-bumper warranties cover 36 months/36,000 miles, powertrain 60 months/60,000 miles). Your first lease would be fully covered; the second lease would be on a new car, also fully covered.

Buying exposes you to out-of-warranty costs in years 4 and 5 (months 37–60, roughly 37,000–60,000 miles). For the vehicles you're considering — Accord, Camry, Mazda6 class — this risk is genuinely low. These are among the most reliable sedans manufactured. At 60,000 miles, you're looking at routine maintenance (oil changes, tire rotation, brake pads, possibly new tires) — not major repairs.

Expected maintenance costs for the purchase scenario over 5 years: ~$3,000–$4,000. That's already baked into the cost comparison above, and buying still wins by $5,000+.

The realistic risk: an out-of-warranty surprise repair in year 4 or 5 costing $1,000–$2,000 (AC compressor, suspension component, electrical issue). On these vehicles, the probability is low but nonzero. Even in the worst case, the purchase still comes out ahead financially.

If repair risk anxiety is high, you can buy an extended warranty at purchase. A reputable manufacturer-backed extended warranty (not third-party) runs $1,200–$1,800 for 5 years/60,000 miles. It's statistically a losing bet on these reliable vehicles, but it's a valid hedge if peace of mind matters.

## The Two-Kids Factor

This is an underappreciated consideration. You mentioned two young children who will inevitably cause interior wear. Leased vehicles are inspected at turn-in, and excess wear charges are a reliable source of frustration and unexpected cost:

- Stained or damaged upholstery: $200–$500 per area
- Scuffs on interior trim: $100–$200
- Exterior door dings (kids opening doors into things): $75–$150 each

You'd face this inspection twice over 5 years with two leases. Budget $500–$1,500 in total for excess wear charges, realistically. You can mitigate this with seat covers and all-weather mats, but you can't eliminate it with small children.

With a purchased vehicle, wear is simply wear. It'll slightly reduce your resale value, but the delta is smaller than lease-return penalties because private buyers and dealers expect reasonable wear on a 5-year-old car.

## Implementation Recommendations

**If you proceed with buying (recommended):**

1. **Get pre-approved** through your credit union or bank before visiting dealers. A 760 score should get you 5.0–6.0% on 60 months. Having a pre-approval in hand is your best negotiating lever.

2. **Target a Honda Accord or Toyota Camry** specifically. The Mazda6 has been discontinued in the US, which will affect parts availability and resale value over time. Accord and Camry hold their value better than almost any other sedans, which strengthens the buy case further.

3. **Choose the 72-month loan if the monthly payment matters.** The additional interest (~$1,500) is a small price for staying within your budget, and it doesn't change the buy-vs-lease calculus.

4. **Skip the extended warranty** unless it's a Honda or Toyota factory-backed plan at a reasonable price ($1,200–$1,500). Third-party warranties are generally poor value.

5. **Invest in protection upfront**: all-weather floor mats, rear seat covers, and paint protection film on high-impact areas. Total cost: ~$300–$500. This preserves resale value far more than it costs.

6. **Set aside $100/month in a car maintenance fund** starting in month 37 (when warranty expires). By month 60, you'll have $2,300 banked for any repair needs — likely more than you'll use.

## Final Note

The lease-vs-buy question generates a lot of debate, but that's usually because people's profiles differ. In your case, the profile is almost textbook "buy": moderate mileage, long hold period, reliable vehicle class, cost-optimization priority, and no desire for perpetual newness. The only real argument for leasing here is monthly payment smoothness, and a 72-month loan or slightly lower purchase price solves that without the $5,000+ cost penalty.

Buy the Accord or Camry. Drive it for 5 years. You'll spend less, own an asset, and have the flexibility to adapt if life changes.
