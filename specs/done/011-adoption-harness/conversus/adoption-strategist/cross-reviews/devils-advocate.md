# Cross-Review: Devil's Advocate Review — by Adoption Strategist

---

### Dangerous Contradictions

- **Build order sequencing: infrastructure-first vs. adoption-first**
  - **devils-advocate claims**: "Resolve the engine extraction question before proceeding" (Actionable Recommendations #5) — framing the Python extraction as a "blocking architectural decision that determines the shape of everything downstream." The devil's advocate treats this as an unresolved technical decision that must be answered first, but implicitly accepts that it comes first by calling it blocking.
  - **adoption-strategist claims**: "Python engine extraction is step 1 is an off-base assumption" (Off-Base Assumptions, third bullet) and "Ship 'Just Ask' as a Claude Code skill first" (Actionable Recommendations #1) — arguing that engine extraction is not a prerequisite for adoption and should be deferred until usage data justifies it. "Just Ask" mode can be implemented as a thin wrapper around the existing SKILL.md runtime today.
  - **Why this is dangerous**: If the synthesis adopts both positions — "resolve the engine question before proceeding" AND "ship Just Ask in the current runtime first" — the team faces a deadlock. The devil's advocate's framing makes engine extraction feel like a gate that must be passed before any other work begins. My framing says the opposite: ship user-facing value first, then extract the engine if validated. If both recommendations land in the final plan without reconciliation, the team will either delay everything for engine extraction (killing adoption velocity) or skip engine extraction and discover downstream that the MCP server and CLI cannot be built without it (wasting the Just Ask validation period).
  - **Suggested resolution**: Both positions can coexist if sequenced correctly. The adoption-strategist should yield on the necessity of engine extraction for the CLI/MCP path, and the devil's advocate should yield on timing — engine extraction is not step 1, it is step 2, after "Just Ask" has been validated in the existing runtime. The compromise: ship "Just Ask" as a Claude Code skill (no engine extraction needed), run validation, and begin engine extraction in parallel once usage data confirms the product direction. This satisfies the devil's advocate's concern about architectural clarity while satisfying the adoption strategist's concern about time-to-users.

- **Quality criteria as a gate vs. quality criteria as an iteration target**
  - **devils-advocate claims**: "Define deliberation quality criteria" (Actionable Recommendations #1, labeled P1) — proposing concrete minimum thresholds before shipping: "the synthesis must surface at least one substantive disagreement," "each agent must reference specific evidence," "the recommendation must acknowledge trade-offs." The devil's advocate frames these as prerequisites: "Without quality criteria, there is no way to iterate on 'just ask' mode."
  - **adoption-strategist claims**: "Ship 'Just Ask' as a Claude Code skill first" (Actionable Recommendations #1) with a validation approach: "validate it with 100 real decisions, and let usage data — not architectural ambition — drive the subsequent build order." My review implicitly treats quality as something learned from real usage, not defined upfront.
  - **Why this is dangerous**: If the synthesis mandates quality criteria as a shipping gate, the team enters a specification loop — defining quality bars for a product no one has used yet, then building to those bars, then discovering the bars were wrong. If the synthesis adopts the adoption strategist's "ship and learn" approach without any quality floor, the team ships a product that may genuinely be indistinguishable from ChatGPT, users notice, and first impressions kill the product. Both failure modes are real.
  - **Suggested resolution**: The devil's advocate is right that some quality floor must exist before shipping — shipping a product that is literally indistinguishable from a single-model response would be worse than not shipping at all. The adoption strategist is right that the quality bar should be minimal and empirical, not exhaustive and theoretical. Compromise: define two heuristic quality checks before shipping "Just Ask" — (1) the synthesis must contain at least one point of genuine disagreement between agents, and (2) the output must structurally differ from a single-model response (i.e., show agent attributions). These are testable in 30 minutes, not months. Then use the 100-decision validation to refine the full quality model the devil's advocate envisions.

- **"Just Ask" lightweight mode: value-preserving or value-destroying?**
  - **devils-advocate claims**: "2-3 agents, 1 round implies adequate deliberation is off-base" (Off-Base Assumptions, second bullet) — arguing that "a 2-agent, 1-round deliberation is structurally identical to asking two models the same question and having a third summarize — which is not what makes conversus valuable." The devil's advocate further argues in Actionable Recommendations #3 that skipping cross-reviews removes the core adversarial mechanism.
  - **adoption-strategist claims**: "Add 'see the debate' to Just Ask output" (Actionable Recommendations #3) and "Answer 'Why not just ask ChatGPT?' in the first user interaction" (Actionable Recommendations #9) — my review accepts the lightweight configuration as workable but insists that the differentiator must be visible in the output, not hidden by simplification.
  - **Why this is dangerous**: These positions pull in opposite directions on a fundamental design question. The devil's advocate says a 2-agent, 1-round run without cross-reviews does not produce conversus-quality output — it is structurally equivalent to multi-model summarization. I say the output format and visibility of the debate is what communicates value. If both positions are adopted, the team faces an impossible design constraint: make "Just Ask" fast and cheap (2-3 agents, 1 round) AND include genuine adversarial pressure (which requires cross-reviews, which the devil's advocate correctly notes would mean 9+ agent launches, not "lightweight"). The proposal's own "fast and cheap" claim collapses.
  - **Suggested resolution**: The devil's advocate's "mini-cross-review" suggestion (Actionable Recommendations #3) is the right compromise — a single combined prompt where agents respond to each other's initial positions within one additional round. This preserves some adversarial pressure without the full N*(N-1) cross-review explosion. The adoption strategist should adopt this design, and the "see the debate" output should show this mini-cross-review as evidence that agents actually engaged with each other's positions. Both reviewers should agree on concrete agent-launch budgets for "Just Ask": perhaps 2 agents + 1 mini-cross-review + 1 synthesis = 4 launches, not 2-3 and not 9.

### Tensions

- **Scope of web app critique: defer vs. separate**
  - **devils-advocate's position**: "Separate the web app into its own spec" (Actionable Recommendations #7) — the devil's advocate recommends removing the web app from the build order and creating a separate spec (012 or later) after the core engine, CLI, and MCP server are stable. The framing is architectural: the web app introduces too many design surfaces (auth, hosting, state management, databases) for a single spec.
  - **adoption-strategist's position**: "Cut the web app from the initial roadmap" (Actionable Recommendations #8) — I recommend removing the web app entirely unless demand is validated by earlier channels. My framing is strategic: the web app should be justified by demonstrated demand, not assumed need. I also argue (Off-Base Assumptions, third bullet) that a Slack bot or ChatGPT plugin might reach non-technical users faster than a custom web app.
  - **Nature of tension**: Both reviews agree the web app should not be in this spec, but for different reasons. The devil's advocate treats the web app as a legitimate future deliverable that needs its own spec. I treat it as a possibly unnecessary deliverable that should only be built if demand materializes. This tension matters because "create a separate spec" implies the web app is on the roadmap — it just needs more design. "Cut it until demand is proven" implies it might never be built. The team's resource allocation and mental model differ significantly between these two framings.
  - **Coordination needed**: The synthesis should adopt my framing (defer pending validation) but acknowledge the devil's advocate's concern (if built, it needs its own spec). The resolution: "The web app is removed from the 011 roadmap. If MCP/CLI adoption reveals demand from non-technical users, a separate spec will be created with the design depth the devil's advocate identifies as necessary."

- **MCP maturity: primary path vs. second channel**
  - **devils-advocate's position**: "MCP as 'primary integration path' without fallback" (Missed Opportunities, eighth bullet) — flags the lack of a fallback strategy if MCP adoption stalls, but does not challenge the eventual primacy of MCP. The devil's advocate's Actionable Recommendation #10 asks for a more precise MCP tool contract, implying MCP will be built and needs rigorous specification.
  - **adoption-strategist's position**: "MCP is the primary integration path is off-base" (Off-Base Assumptions, first bullet) — I argue MCP should be the second distribution channel, not the primary one. The primary path for the first 100 users is the existing Claude Code skill. I also recommend (Actionable Recommendations #4) reordering MCP before CLI but after Claude Code skill validation.
  - **Nature of tension**: The devil's advocate accepts MCP's eventual primacy and focuses on making it robust (tool contracts, fallback strategies). I challenge whether MCP should be called "primary" at all given current adoption levels. These are not contradictory — both want MCP built — but they produce different investment signals. If MCP is "primary," it gets the most engineering attention. If it is "second channel," it gets built after Claude Code validation and with less initial polish.
  - **Coordination needed**: Agree on terminology: MCP is the "primary programmatic integration path" (for tools and IDEs), while the Claude Code skill is the "primary adoption path" (for first users). This resolves the semantic disagreement without changing either review's substantive recommendation.

- **Cost controls: user-facing vs. system-level**
  - **devils-advocate's position**: "Add cost estimation and budget controls" (Actionable Recommendations #4, P1) — proposes pre-execution cost estimates with user confirmation ("This deliberation will use approximately X tokens across Y API calls, estimated cost: $Z. Proceed?") and programmatic budget caps for SDK/CI usage.
  - **adoption-strategist's position**: "No pricing or sustainability model mentioned" (Missed Opportunities, eighth bullet) — I flag the absence of a business model but frame it as a strategic question (open source vs. freemium vs. SaaS) rather than a UX feature. My Actionable Recommendations #6 addresses the open-source strategy but does not propose per-run cost visibility.
  - **Nature of tension**: The devil's advocate treats cost as a user experience problem — surprise bills kill retention. I treat cost as a business model problem — the team needs to decide what is free and what is paid. Both are correct but operate at different levels. A per-run cost estimator is useless if the business model has not been decided (estimate against whose API key? the user's? a hosted service's?). But a business model decision is academic if users churn from surprise costs before the business model matters.
  - **Coordination needed**: Sequence these: the devil's advocate's per-run cost visibility is needed immediately for the "Just Ask" Claude Code skill (where users pay their own API costs). The adoption strategist's business model question becomes relevant at the web app / hosted service stage. Both are valid; they apply at different phases.

- **Competitive differentiation: structural vs. visible**
  - **devils-advocate's position**: "Address the 'just another ChatGPT wrapper' risk head-on" (Actionable Recommendations #9) — proposes a "Differentiation" section with a side-by-side example showing structural differences between conversus output and single-model output. The devil's advocate frames differentiation as a specification problem: "If the differences are not visible in a side-by-side comparison, the product has a positioning problem that no amount of engineering will fix."
  - **adoption-strategist's position**: "Answer 'Why not just ask ChatGPT?' in the first user interaction" (Actionable Recommendations #9) and "Add 'see the debate' to Just Ask output" (Actionable Recommendations #3) — I frame differentiation as an output design problem: the product must show the debate, not just the conclusion. The differentiation is communicated through the output format, not through documentation.
  - **Nature of tension**: The devil's advocate wants the spec to prove differentiation exists (via a side-by-side example in the spec document). I want the product to communicate differentiation to users (via output format at runtime). These are complementary but could conflict if the team invests in spec-level documentation of differentiation instead of building the "see the debate" output that actually communicates it to users.
  - **Coordination needed**: Do both. The spec should include a side-by-side example (devil's advocate) to validate that the differentiation is real, and the product should surface the debate in every output (adoption strategist) to communicate that differentiation to users. The side-by-side example is a design artifact; the "see the debate" output is a product feature. Neither substitutes for the other.

- **Agent auto-generation: specify the algorithm vs. ship and iterate**
  - **devils-advocate's position**: "Define what 'auto-generates 2-4 appropriate agents' means concretely" (Actionable Recommendations #8, P2) — proposes evaluating at least two agent generation approaches (classification model, prompt-based generation, rule-based matching) and selecting one with rationale before building.
  - **adoption-strategist's position**: My review does not explicitly address the agent generation algorithm. The closest position is the recommendation to use the decision framework's existing heuristics (`999-decision-framework/spec.md, L309-343`) as the foundation for auto-generation (Actionable Recommendations #1, referencing spec 003 heuristics).
  - **Nature of tension**: The devil's advocate wants algorithm selection before implementation. My review implicitly assumes the spec 003 heuristics are sufficient — classify the problem type, map to interest patterns, generate agents accordingly. This is effectively option (a) in the devil's advocate's list (classification-based), but I never explicitly evaluated alternatives. If the synthesis adopts the devil's advocate's recommendation to evaluate multiple approaches, it adds design work before shipping. If it adopts my implicit position, it ships faster but may discover the heuristic approach is insufficient.
  - **Coordination needed**: Use the spec 003 heuristics as the initial implementation (ship fast), but document the devil's advocate's alternative approaches as iteration options if the heuristic approach produces poor agent configurations in the 100-decision validation.

### Safe Agreements

- **"Just Ask" mode is the highest-priority feature, and its differentiator must be visible**
  - **Shared position**: Both reviews identify "Just Ask" mode as the critical adoption feature and both warn that it risks being indistinguishable from single-model responses if the differentiation is not surfaced. The devil's advocate states: "the proposal never confronts the central tension: the features that make conversus genuinely valuable...are exactly the features most likely to be gutted or diluted by the 'just ask' simplification" (Executive Summary). The adoption strategist states: "Without this, conversus is indistinguishable from asking ChatGPT" (Missed Opportunities, fourth bullet) and recommends a "see the debate" output section (Actionable Recommendations #3).
  - **Combined evidence**: The devil's advocate provides structural evidence — a 2-agent, 1-round deliberation without cross-reviews is architecturally equivalent to multi-model summarization. The adoption strategist provides market evidence — users will not adopt a tool that looks like "ChatGPT with extra steps." Together, these establish that the differentiation problem is both technically real (the simplified architecture genuinely might not produce better output) and strategically fatal (even if it does produce better output, users will not perceive it without visible evidence). The devil's advocate's proposed quality criteria and the adoption strategist's proposed "see the debate" output are complementary solutions to the same problem.
  - **Confidence level**: High. This is the single most important design decision in the spec. Both reviews converge on it from independent perspectives (architectural integrity vs. market positioning), and both provide actionable recommendations that reinforce each other. The synthesis must address this or the product fails.

- **The web app should be removed from this spec's scope**
  - **Shared position**: Both reviews recommend removing the web app from the current spec. The devil's advocate states: "Remove the web app from this spec's build order. Create a separate spec (012 or later) for the web app after the core engine, CLI, and MCP server are stable" (Actionable Recommendations #7). The adoption strategist states: "Remove the web app from this proposal entirely. If adoption via Claude Code skill, MCP, and CLI validates demand from non-technical users, revisit a web app as a separate spec" (Actionable Recommendations #8).
  - **Combined evidence**: The devil's advocate provides architectural evidence — the web app introduces five major design surfaces (authentication, hosting, state management, real-time streaming, database design) that are not addressed in the current spec. The adoption strategist provides strategic evidence — the web app is the highest-cost, highest-risk item, and building it before validating demand is a "build the product before finding the market" mistake. Both cite the proposal's own build order placing the web app at step 7 of 8, suggesting even the proposal implicitly recognizes it is not urgent. The devil's advocate further notes the architect agent in the conversus.yml explicitly flags web app complexity.
  - **Confidence level**: High. Both reviews arrive at the same recommendation from independent reasoning. The only difference — whether to create a separate spec now (devil's advocate) or defer pending demand validation (adoption strategist) — is a minor sequencing question, not a disagreement on the core recommendation.

- **The proposal's open questions are more blocking than presented**
  - **Shared position**: Both reviews identify the proposal's open questions (L129-138) as understated in their impact. The devil's advocate states: "several of them, if answered differently than the proposal implicitly assumes, would invalidate entire sections of the architecture" (Executive Summary). The adoption strategist identifies specific open questions that must be resolved before investment — particularly the open-source strategy (L133, addressed in Actionable Recommendations #6) and MCP maturity (L135, addressed in Actionable Recommendations #10).
  - **Combined evidence**: The devil's advocate demonstrates this by showing that open question 1 (engine extraction strategy) determines the entire downstream architecture, and the build order proceeds as if it has been answered. The adoption strategist demonstrates this by showing that open question 3 (SaaS vs. self-hostable) determines whether the web app is a product or a demo, affecting every investment decision. Together, these establish that the spec's honesty about open questions is a double-edged sword: intellectually honest, but practically insufficient, because the rest of the spec treats the questions as rhetorical rather than blocking.
  - **Confidence level**: Medium. Both reviews agree that the open questions need resolution, but they prioritize different questions (devil's advocate: engine extraction; adoption strategist: business model). The synthesis should force resolution of the top three blocking questions before approving the build order, but the ranking of which three are most blocking requires deliberation between the two perspectives.
